Auction Scene

On March 1, 2026, the Hong Kong Convention and Exhibition Centre hosted the Lunar New Year license plate auction. Among 49 plates, the most eye-catching was Lot 26: the only single-letter plate, "H".

The "H" plate started with a reserve price of HK$5,000. Right after the auctioneer announced the starting bid, a bidder called out HK$5 million, pushing the price a thousand times higher. The auctioneer quickly adjusted the bidding increments from HK$10,000 all the way up to HK$200,000.

Multiple heavyweight bidders took turns raising paddles, and the price quickly exceeded HK$10 million. After 40 minutes and 90 rounds of intense bidding, Bidder No. 135 called out HK$20 million.

"HK$20 million first—HK$20 million second—HK$20 million, third!" The auctioneer's gavel came down firmly, finalizing the price.

The single-letter "H" plate sold for a staggering HK$20 million, making it the fourth most expensive license plate in Hong Kong history.

Why would tycoons spend a fortune on a small piece of metal? What are they really paying for? Where does this huge sum of money end up? Behind this seemingly simple transaction of wealth, why does it raise questions about institutional fairness? All of this begins with this license plate, slowly unraveling.

Logic of Wealth Concentration: To understand why someone would pay HK$20 million for a single letter, we must first look at Hong Kong's wealth structure. According to a UBS report, Hong Kong's Gini coefficient is 0.63, far above the international warning level of 0.4, making it one of the most unequal economies in the world. When wealth is concentrated among a tiny elite, prices no longer reflect just the value of a commodity, but also the ordering of purchasing desires.

This logic of concentration is directly reflected in license plate auctions. Single-letter plates are extremely rare but account for the majority of transaction value. In the 2026 Lunar New Year auction, single-letter plates made up only 3% of the lots but contributed 67% of the total transaction amount.

Feng Shui & Symbolism

But wealth alone is not enough. Why this particular form—a license plate? And why the letter "H"?

In Hong Kong, feng shui is not mere superstition but a system incorporated into business decisions and architectural designs. Examples include the bronze lion placement at HSBC Plaza and the "sharp blade" design of the Bank of China Tower.

License plates are no exception. Letters and numbers are endowed with meanings based on the five elements, shapes, or homophones. For instance, the number 8 represents prosperity, and the letter W symbolizes victory. The letter "H" in this auction corresponds to the Year of the Horse ("Horse"), symbolizing immediate success.

When these two characteristics overlap, the billion-dollar plate ceases to be a curiosity and becomes a consumption pattern aligned with the spirit of the times.

Scarce Assets & Alternative Investments

Spending HK$20 million on a single letter may seem crazy to outsiders, but in the eyes of Hong Kong's tycoons, it might not be a bad deal.

The value of a sky-high plate first lies in its scarcity. A mansion can be bought in installments, a yacht can be rented, but a single-letter license plate is a truly non-reproducible rarity. The uniqueness and visual immediacy of a license plate make it an excellent choice for low-key showing off among the wealthy. Without self-introduction, a plate can speak for itself as a status symbol.

Another part of the answer lies in Hong Kong's deeply ingrained feng shui culture.

Beyond identity and feng shui, there is also a rational investment consideration. License plates are not consumer goods but tradable assets. Research by scholars such as Terence Chong of the Chinese University of Hong Kong indicates that three-digit plates containing lucky numbers like "8" command a premium of 95%, and the worse the stock market performs, the higher the premium. For every 1% drop in the Hang Seng Index, the price of lucky license plates rises by 11%. Spending HK$20 million today might not lead to a loss if sold three years later.

Fund Flow

Since license plates have a stable market, where does the auction revenue go?

Traditional license plates (e.g., AB1234) all go into the Government Lotteries Fund after auction, used for rehabilitation services, family support, community kitchens, and other public welfare purposes.

According to Social Welfare Department data, the Government Lotteries Fund's total revenue in 2024-2025 was approximately HK$2.3 billion, with allocations primarily directed to elderly services (60.24%) and rehabilitation services (31.67%). License plate auctions are one of the stable sources of revenue.

In contrast, revenue from personalized plates (such as the single-letter "H") goes to the government treasury (general revenue). The Personalized Vehicle Registration Marks Scheme was first proposed by then-Financial Secretary Henry Tang in his 2004 budget and officially launched in 2006.

Institutional Questions

Why are the flows of these two types of plate auctions so different?

In 2005, Henry Tang, then Acting Chief Executive, clearly announced that the net proceeds from the "Personalized License Plate Scheme" would be used for poverty alleviation. Chan Kam-lam, then Chairman of the LegCo's Bills Committee on the Personalized Vehicle Registration Marks Ordinance, said at the time: "Using it for poverty alleviation or the Lotteries Fund, essentially helping those in need, is fine." However, this commitment had a time limit—the government only guaranteed to set aside HK$60 million annually from personalized plate revenues for poverty alleviation during the five years from 2006/07 to 2010/11.

In fact, the actual revenue from personalized plates fell far short of expectations. The government initially expected to generate HK$70 million per year, but the total revenue over the first five fiscal years was less than HK$200 million, averaging about HK$38 million per year. The government had to subsidize the difference—about HK$20 million per year—to maintain the HK$60 million poverty alleviation commitment.

Since the end of that period, revenue from personalized license plate auctions has all gone into the government treasury as general revenue and is no longer specifically used for poverty alleviation or social welfare.

Critics have raised questions.

Democratic Party legislator Wong Sing-chi criticized that the government's introduction of the personalized license plate scheme would inevitably lead to a significant reduction in the Lotteries Fund's income, forcing funded agencies to compete with the Social Welfare Department for resources. He called on the government to establish additional mechanisms to fill the reduced income of the Lotteries Fund.

A 2020 HK01 article, discussing the risk of the Lotteries Fund running dry, also mentioned contributions from the personalized plate scheme, questioning the appropriateness of the fund's allocation and management.

This small piece of metal, finally hammered down at HK$20 million, appears to be a tycoon's transaction, but it is also a focal point for many social issues. It shows us that the billion-dollar plate is not an oddity, but a microcosm of Hong Kong's extreme wealth concentration, feng shui culture permeating business decisions, and the shift of conspicuous consumption toward alternative investments.
